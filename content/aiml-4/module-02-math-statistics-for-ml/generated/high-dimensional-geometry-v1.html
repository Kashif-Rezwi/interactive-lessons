UNIT 1 · THE DIMENSIONALITY OF EVERYTHING

Every image, sentence, and click is already a huge vector

CLASS CORE Lede: modern machine learning does not draw pictures of data — it computes with points that live in spaces with thousands of coordinates, and that changes what "near", "far", and "between" mean.

A dimension (or feature) is one measurable coordinate of a data point. An image is not "a picture" to an algorithm: it is one number per pixel-channel value. A 256×256 RGB image therefore has 256 × 256 × 3 = 196,608 dimensions source. A text embedding — a learned vector that represents an item's meaning — typically has 768 dimensions (BERT), 1536 (OpenAI embeddings), or 384–1024 (sentence-transformer families) source. A recommender system represents every user and product as a dense latent vector too source.

d = w · h · c
  • w — image width in pixels
  • h — image height in pixels
  • c — channels per pixel (3 for RGB, 1 for grayscale, 4 for RGBA)
  • d — the dimension of the space one image lives in
  • Why it makes sense: every (row, column, channel) triple is one independent readable number, so the count of triples is the count of coordinates.
  • How to read the result: d is the address length of each data point — a 256×256×3 image is a point in ℝ196,608.

DEEP DIVE constructed example Here is the number that should bother you: if a grid put just 2 values on every axis of ℝ196,608, visiting every point at one billion points per second would take about 1059,000 years. No dataset, no computer, no century touches that. Yet the images we actually see occupy a vanishingly thin slice of that space. The rest of this lesson is about the consequences of that fact. supplemental

UNIT 2 · THE CURSE OF DIMENSIONALITY

Cover a fixed slice of every axis — and the covered space vanishes

CLASS CORE Lede: the curse of dimensionality is not one problem but a family of failures — vanishing coverage, sparsity, and exponential data hunger — that all come from the same exponential arithmetic.

Start with the object the notes use: the unit hypercube, the d-dimensional box where every coordinate lies in [0, 1]. Its volume is 1d = 1 in every dimension source — the cube itself is harmless, and it is worth being precise here: what explodes with dimension is not the cube's volume but the number of small boxes needed to cover it and the fraction of it that any fixed-size neighborhood covers supplemental (the notes' phrase "volume grows exponentially" is tightened here — tagged as an artifact correction).

Suppose you want a neighborhood that covers a fraction ε of every axis — say ε = 0.01, "the 1% local neighborhood." In d dimensions that neighborhood covers

V(ε, d) = εd
  • ε — the fraction of one axis your neighborhood spans
  • d — the dimension of the space
  • εd — the product of d copies of ε: the covered share of total volume
  • Why it makes sense: volume in d dimensions multiplies per-axis shares — the box is ε wide in every one of d independent directions.
  • How to read the result: ε = 0.01 gives 0.0001 in 2-D, 10−20 in 10-D, and effectively 0 in 100-D — the numbers from the notes' table, reproduced live below.
N(ε, d) = (1/ε)d
  • 1/ε — how many ε-sized boxes fit along one axis
  • (1/ε)d — boxes needed to tile the whole d-cube at that resolution
  • Why it makes sense: tiling multiplies counts per axis, just as volume did.
  • How to read the result: at ε = 0.1, going from 2-D (100 boxes) to 12-D (1012 boxes) multiplies the work by ten billion — with a dataset that grew not at all.

CLASS CORE The same arithmetic explains sparsity. One million points sounds like a lot — until you ask what fraction of [0,1]1000 they occupy: essentially zero. High-dimensional datasets are mostly empty space source. And the data hunger follows: if 10 points per axis felt comfortable in 1-D, the same per-axis comfort needs 102 in 2-D, 1010 in 10-D, and 10100 in 100-D source. Treat those as an illustrative sketch of the growth rate (an honest statement of exponential sample complexity), not an exact law — constants depend on the task supplemental.

UNIT 3 · DISTANCE CONCENTRATION

In high dimensions, nearest and farthest look the same

CLASS CORE Lede: the question the notes pose — "how do distances behave in high dimensions?" — has a destabilizing answer: distances lose their discriminative power, everything ends up roughly equally far, and neighborhood-based algorithms quietly lose their footing source.

FOUNDATION The tool for asking the question is the Euclidean distance, the straight-line length between two points:

‖x − y‖ = √( Σi (xi − yi)² )
  • xi, yi — the i-th coordinates of points x and y
  • Σi — the sum over all d dimensions
  • √ — the square root converting squared length to length
  • Why it makes sense: per-axis differences are squared (so sign is lost and big gaps dominate), summed, and rooted.
  • How to read the result: a length in the same units as the coordinates; for random points in [0,1]d it grows like √(d/6).

DEEP DIVE constructed example Here is a number we will use twice: for two independent uniform points in [0,1]d, each axis contributes an average squared gap of E[(X−Y)²] = 2·Var(X) = 2·(1/12) = 1/6 (the variance of a uniform [0,1] variable is 1/12; independence makes the per-axis contributions simply add). Summing d of them and taking the root:

E‖X − Y‖² = d/6  ⇒  typical ‖X − Y‖ ≈ √(d/6)
  • E — the expected value, i.e. the long-run average over random draws
  • d/6 — d axes, each contributing an average squared gap of 1/6
  • Why it makes sense: squared gaps of independent axes add (linearity of expectation).
  • How to read the result: at d = 1000 the typical gap is ≈ √166.7 ≈ 12.91 — right where the notes' code experiment lands (13.0006 in its recorded run). Distances grow like √d, but that is not the problem — the spread around that typical value is. supplemental

FOUNDATION Why does this happen? Each axis contributes a small random piece to the squared distance. Adding many independent random pieces does not produce a wild total — it produces a predictable one, because fluctuations partially cancel: deviations above and below each axis's average tend to offset across dimensions. This is the mechanism behind the Central Limit Theorem, and the phenomenon has a name — concentration of measure — that Unit 5 defines properly and turns into a tool. Consider that a promise: the informal explanation you just read becomes quantitative there. source

CLASS CORE The casualty is KNN (k-nearest neighbors), which classifies a point by voting among its closest training points. KNN's entire logic assumes "nearby points are meaningfully more similar than far ones." Under concentration that premise fails: the nearest neighbor is barely closer than the farthest, so "nearest" carries almost no information — classification degrades and retrieval becomes unstable source. The same collapse weakens high-dimensional clustering and density estimation, where "dense region" stops being well-defined source.

3. Explain in your own words. Why do distances concentrate when you add up many small independent random contributions? Write your answer from memory, then compare with the model answer and grade yourself honestly.

A distance is built axis by axis: each of the d squared gaps is a small random number with its own ups and downs. When you add many independent random numbers, their fluctuations partially cancel — a gap above average on one axis is offset by a gap below average on another — so the total lands close to the sum of the averages and its relative spread shrinks as d grows. Every pairwise distance therefore sits in a narrow band around the same typical value, and the difference between "near" and "far" loses meaning. The relative spread of the sum shrinks roughly like 1/√d — Unit 5 makes this quantitative.
CONNECT · WHAT BREAKS ML LINK

KNN classifiers, nearest-neighbor retrieval, distance-based anomaly detection, and k-means-style clustering all lean on distance contrast. The notes' verdict — "distance itself becomes less meaningful" — is not a reason to give up on geometry; it is the reason the choice of geometry (next unit) and the shape of the space (Unit 5) matter so much source.

UNIT 4 · METRIC CHOICE: EUCLIDEAN VS COSINE

Cosine compares direction and ignores magnitude

CLASS CORE Lede: if distances blur together, the way out is to measure what concentration does not flatten — orientation. That is why the metric comparison in the notes ends with cosine as the embedding-workhorse source.

FOUNDATION Two ingredients. The dot product x·y multiplies matching coordinates and adds: it is large when the vectors point the same way. The cosine similarity normalizes that alignment by the vectors' lengths, leaving a pure measure of the angle between them (the notes' LaTeX for this formula is corrupted in the source; this is the intended equation, tagged as an artifact correction):

cos θ = (x · y) / (‖x‖ ‖y‖)
  • x · y — the dot product: Σi xiyi
  • ‖x‖, ‖y‖ — the lengths of the two vectors
  • θ — the angle between them
  • Why it makes sense: dividing by the lengths strips away "how big" and keeps only "how aligned" — cos θ = 1 same direction, 0 perpendicular, −1 opposite.
  • How to read the result: a pure number in [−1, 1]; for embeddings, closer to 1 usually means semantically closer.

DEEP DIVE constructed example The lab's surprise line: doubling L leaves cos θ untouched but changes the Euclidean distance — scale invariance is the whole trick. And the two metrics are secretly the same question once lengths are removed. Normalize both vectors to unit vectors:

x̂ = x / ‖x‖
  • x — any nonzero vector
  • ‖x‖ — its length
  • x̂ — the unit vector pointing the same way (length 1); this is the normalization the notes prescribe for embeddings
‖x̂ − ŷ‖² = 2 − 2 cos θ
  • x̂, ŷ — the unit-vector versions of x and y
  • θ — the angle between them
  • Why it makes sense: ‖x̂ − ŷ‖² = ‖x̂‖² + ‖ŷ‖² − 2 x̂·ŷ = 1 + 1 − 2cos θ, since each unit vector has length 1 and x̂·ŷ = cos θ.
  • How to read the result: on normalized vectors, Euclidean distance and cosine disagreement are the same measurement — sorting by either gives the identical ranking. This is exactly why "normalize, then use Euclidean" and "use cosine" are interchangeable inside vector-search systems supplemental.

UNIT 5 · CONCENTRATION OF MEASURE

Random high-dimensional vectors all have nearly the same length

CLASS CORE Lede: concentration of measure is the deep rule behind everything so far: in high dimensions, random quantities stop wandering and hug their mean source.

FOUNDATION The standard model for studying this is the standard Gaussian vector: x ~ N(0, Id) means each of the d coordinates is drawn independently from the bell curve with mean 0 and variance 1 — no coordinate influences another. A Monte-Carlo simulation measures such quantities by drawing many random samples and looking at the resulting distribution; that is exactly what the lab below does, live, with a seeded generator so every number is reproducible supplemental.

CLASS CORE The notes claim the vector's length is nearly constant — and give its value. The source writes ‖x‖ ≈ d, but that is a transcription typo: the correct statement (verifiable in the lab below) is source, artifact correction:

‖x‖ ≈ √d   for x ~ N(0, Id)
  • x — a Gaussian vector with d independent standard coordinates
  • N(0, Id) — the standard Gaussian distribution in d dimensions
  • ‖x‖ — the norm: the vector's length, √(Σ xi²)
  • √d — the typical (root-mean-square) length: each of d coordinates contributes variance 1
  • Why it makes sense: ‖x‖² = Σ xi² is a sum of d unit-variance terms, so it averages to d — the length therefore orbits √d.
  • How to read the result: at d = 1000, essentially every random vector has length between roughly 30 and 33 (√1000 ≈ 31.62). Almost all vectors have nearly the same length source.
σ/μ ≈ 1/√(2d)
  • μ — the mean of ‖x‖ (approximately √d)
  • σ — the standard deviation of ‖x‖
  • σ/μ — the relative spread: how big the fluctuations are compared with the value itself
  • Why it makes sense: the sum ‖x‖² has relative spread ~√(2/d); the square root softens it slightly.
  • How to read the result: at d = 1000 the relative spread is ≈ 2.2%; at d = 1,000,000 it would be ≈ 0.07%. "Concentration" is this number shrinking supplemental.

CLASS CORE Now the geometric picture the notes draw. Where does a random point of the d-ball sit? In 2-D, area spreads throughout the disk. In high dimensions, a point at radius r·R (with r < 1) is missing volume in every one of d directions at once — and doing that in all d directions together is exponentially unlikely. The result: most of the volume of a high-dimensional sphere lies in a thin shell near its surface; the interior contributes almost nothing. High-dimensional spheres are mostly hollow source. This is the thin shell phenomenon — and it is the payoff of Unit 3's promise: two random points of the ball both live in the shell at radius ≈ R, so the angle between them — not their radius — controls their distance, and typical distances land in a narrow band. Concentration explains distance concentration, neighborhood instability, and why normalization (Unit 4) is so effective: lengths were never where the information was source.

DEEP DIVE supplemental Statistically, the moral is broader than lengths: high-dimensional randomness becomes structured. Quantities that are sums of many weakly-dependent contributions — distances, dot products, attention scores — all concentrate. That is why the notes call this "one of the deepest ideas in high-dimensional geometry" source.

EXTENSION · where the 1/√(2d) comes from (optional)

‖x‖² = Σ xi² is a sum of d independent squared standard Gaussians — a Chi-square(d) variable with mean d and variance 2d. The standard deviation of ‖x‖² is √(2d), a relative spread of √(2d)/d = √2/√d around d. Since ‖x‖ = √(‖x‖²) changes slowly (square root) compared with ‖x‖², the length's relative spread works out to ≈ 1/√(2d). The lab's measured σ/μ tracks this prediction — compare them live.

3. Explain in your own words. Why does the thin shell explain distance concentration? Write your answer from memory, then compare with the model answer.

A random point of a high-d ball is almost never deep inside it: volume concentrates near the surface, so essentially every point — and therefore every pair of points — sits at radius ≈ √d (for Gaussian vectors, literally so). When both points of a pair have (nearly) the same radius, the law of cosines says their distance is determined almost entirely by the angle between them. Angles between random directions also concentrate, so all pairwise distances fall into a narrow band: nearest ≈ farthest. The shell removes radius as a source of variation, and concentration removes the rest.
CONNECT · WHY NORMALIZATION WORKS ML LINK

The notes list four things the shell explains: distance concentration, unstable neighborhoods, embedding-geometry behavior, and the effectiveness of normalization source. The last one is the actionable one: if all meaningful variation lives in direction, removing magnitude costs nothing and buys stability — Unit 4's x̂ = x/‖x‖, now with a reason.

UNIT 6 · FROM CURSE TO CRAFT

Know the failure, pick the fix, and see where modern AI leans on the geometry

CLASS CORE Lede: the four effects you have now measured — exponential space, sparsity, equalized distances, concentrated lengths — arrive together, and every practical rule in this unit is a response to one of them source.

CLASS CORE The notes' summary of what simultaneously happens as dimension increases: (1) space expands exponentially — volume explodes; (2) data becomes sparse — points occupy a negligible fraction of the space; (3) distances become similar — nearest ≈ farthest; (4) values concentrate — norms stabilize around their mean. The final result: everything becomes statistically uniform, and the classical assumptions (nearby points are meaningful, local neighborhoods are informative, distances discriminate) break source.

CLASS CORE The fixes, in the order the notes give them:

1. Feature selection. Remove irrelevant features, noisy dimensions, and redundant variables; the notes promise better generalization, interpretability, and computational efficiency source. This attacks the curse at its root — fewer axes, less exponential space to cover.

2. Dimensionality reduction. Compress information, preserve structure, remove redundancy source. You know the flagship tool from Class 2: PCA projects onto maximum-variance directions — the notes' demo reduces 100 points living in 1000 dimensions to 10 components source. What the notes do not say, and what you can now ask: how much of the data survives such a projection? That is a variance budget:

R(k) = Σi≤k λi / Σi λi
  • λi — the variance carried by the i-th principal direction (its eigenvalue, from Class 2)
  • k — how many directions you keep
  • R(k) — the retained fraction of total variance
  • Why it makes sense: PCA's directions are sorted by variance, so R(k) is the share of the data's spread your k directions explain.
  • How to read the result: R(10) = 0.9 means "ten numbers per point carry 90% of what varied" — that is compression with a price tag you can read. supplemental

Semantic embeddings. SYNTHESIS · THE WHOLE PICTURE

Four effects, one exponential arithmetic

Covering a fixed slice of every axis costs εd — so space explodes (Unit 2), data becomes sparse, and sample demands grow exponentially. Sums of many small random gaps concentrate — so distances equalize (Unit 3), lengths pin to √d, and the sphere hollows into a shell (Unit 5). Cosine survives by measuring the one thing concentration leaves alone: direction (Unit 4). And the craft — feature selection, PCA's variance budget, regularization, normalization — is the set of moves that either shrinks d or chooses metrics that respect the geometry (Unit 6) source.

GLOSSARY · EVERY TERM, SIX FIELDS

Terms used in this lesson

dimension
Simple: one measurable coordinate of a data point. Precise: an axis of the vector space a data point lives in; the count of axes is d. Intuition: the length of a point's address. Example: a 256×256 RGB image has 196,608 dimensions. Related: feature, embedding. In practice/ML: "dimensionality" of models and embeddings is a core design choice.
feature
Simple: one input quantity a model can read. Precise: a single measurable attribute represented as one coordinate of the input vector. Intuition: one question about the item ("how bright is this pixel?"). Example: pixel intensity, word count, sensor reading. Related: dimension, feature selection. In practice/ML: feature engineering and feature explosion both live here.
embedding
Simple: a learned vector that represents an item's meaning. Precise: a point in a learned latent space where geometric closeness encodes semantic similarity. Intuition: meaning as a location. Example: BERT's 768-dim sentence vectors; OpenAI's 1536-dim embeddings. Related: latent space, semantic similarity. In practice/ML: search, recommendation, RAG.
recommender system
Simple: predicts which items a user will want. Precise: a model representing users and items as latent vectors whose alignment predicts preference. Intuition: "people like you liked this." Example: Netflix/Spotify suggestions. Related: embedding, dot product. In practice/ML: industrial-scale high-d similarity.
curse of dimensionality
Simple: the set of things that go wrong as dimension grows. Precise: the family of phenomena — vanishing coverage (ε^d), sparsity, exponential sample complexity, distance concentration — arising from exponential scaling of volume with d. Intuition: data can never fill a space that grows by multiplication. Example: a 1% neighborhood covers 0.0001 of a 2-D cube but ~10⁻²⁰ of a 10-D one. Related: sparsity, distance concentration. In practice/ML: motivates dimensionality reduction and metric choice.
unit hypercube
Simple: the d-dimensional box with all coordinates between 0 and 1. Precise: [0,1]d; volume 1d = 1 in every dimension. Intuition: the standard "room" that data is placed in. Example: random uniform data lives there. Related: coverage, curse of dimensionality. In practice/ML: normalized feature ranges often map to it.
sparsity
Simple: fixed data occupies almost none of the available space. Precise: for fixed N, the fraction of [0,1]d within any fixed distance of the data shrinks toward 0 as d grows. Intuition: mostly empty space. Example: a million points in 1000-D cover essentially zero of the cube. Related: curse of dimensionality, sample complexity. In practice/ML: why high-d density estimates and clustering weaken.
sample complexity
Simple: how much data a task needs. Precise: the number of samples required to achieve a fixed statistical quality; here it grows exponentially with d. Intuition: every new axis multiplies the homework. Example: the 10 → 10² → 10¹⁰ per-axis sketch. Related: sparsity, curse of dimensionality. In practice/ML: why more features demand exponentially more data.
Euclidean distance
Simple: straight-line distance between two points. Precise: ‖x − y‖ = √(Σᵢ (xᵢ − yᵢ)²). Intuition: how far you would fly, ignoring walls. Example: ‖(3,4) − (0,0)‖ = 5. Related: Manhattan distance, cosine similarity. In practice/ML: default metric of k-means and k-NN; unstable in high d.
Manhattan distance
Simple: city-block distance — add up per-axis gaps. Precise: ‖x − y‖₁ = Σᵢ |xᵢ − yᵢ|. Intuition: walking a grid, not flying. Example: |3−0| + |4−0| = 7. Related: Euclidean distance. In practice/ML: still concentration-affected in high d (source's table).
Monte-Carlo simulation
Simple: measure something by simulating it many times at random. Precise: estimating a distribution or expectation by repeated random sampling. Intuition: poll the randomness instead of solving the integral. Example: this lesson's distance and norm histograms. Related: expectation, Gaussian distribution. In practice/ML: evaluation, A/B analysis, Bayesian methods.
KNN (k-nearest neighbors)
Simple: classify a point by voting among its closest training points. Precise: a non-parametric method predicting from the labels of the k training points with smallest distance to the query. Intuition: "tell me who your neighbors are." Example: k = 5 vote among five closest images. Related: Euclidean distance, distance concentration. In practice/ML: fails as distance contrast collapses (Unit 3).
Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
Simple: sums of many small random things behave predictably. Precise: the normalized sum of many independent random variables converges to a Gaussian; its spread grows like √n while its mean grows like n. Intuition: fluctuations cancel across many contributions. Example: 1000 per-axis squared gaps summing to a tight band near d/6. Related: concentration of measure. In practice/ML: the mechanism behind distance concentration.
concentration of measure
Simple: in high dimensions, random quantities hug their mean. Precise: the phenomenon that functions of many weakly-dependent random inputs have relative fluctuations shrinking as dimension grows (e.g., σ/μ ≈ 1/√(2d) for Gaussian norms). Intuition: high-dimensional randomness is surprisingly stable. Example: nearly all Gaussian vectors in 1000-D have length 30–33. Related: thin shell, CLT. In practice/ML: explains distance concentration and normalization's effectiveness.
dot product
Simple: multiply matching coordinates and add. Precise: x·y = Σᵢ xᵢyᵢ = ‖x‖‖y‖cos θ. Intuition: an alignment meter — big when vectors point the same way. Example: (1,2)·(2,1) = 4. Related: cosine similarity, attention. In practice/ML: the core operation of similarity search and attention.
cosine similarity
Simple: how aligned two vectors are, ignoring their lengths. Precise: cos θ = (x·y)/(‖x‖‖y‖) ∈ [−1, 1]. Intuition: compare directions, not sizes. Example: random [0,1]¹⁰⁰⁰ pairs score ≈ 0.75 (positive orthant). Related: dot product, unit vector, scale invariance. In practice/ML: the default metric for embedding retrieval.
scale invariance
Simple: unchanged when a vector is stretched. Precise: cos(αx, y) = cos(x, y) for any α > 0. Intuition: the angle does not care how long the arrow is. Example: doubling every coordinate of x leaves cos(x, y) fixed. Related: cosine similarity, unit vector. In practice/ML: why cosine removes magnitude instability.
unit vector / normalization
Simple: a vector scaled to length 1. Precise: x̂ = x/‖x‖ with ‖x̂‖ = 1 (x ≠ 0). Intuition: keep the direction, drop the size. Example: (3,4) normalizes to (0.6, 0.8). Related: scale invariance, ‖x̂ − ŷ‖² = 2 − 2cos θ. In practice/ML: standard preprocessing before vector search.
positive orthant
Simple: the region where every coordinate is ≥ 0. Precise: {x : xᵢ ≥ 0 for all i}. Intuition: one corner of the space that all nonnegative data shares. Example: pixel intensities, counts, the notes' random experiment. Related: cosine similarity. In practice/ML: explains why random nonnegative vectors have high cosine (≈ 0.75).
norm
Simple: a vector's length. Precise: ‖x‖ = √(Σᵢ xᵢ²) (the Euclidean norm). Intuition: distance from the origin. Example: ‖(3,4)‖ = 5; Gaussian vectors in d dims have ‖x‖ ≈ √d. Related: unit vector, concentration of measure. In practice/ML: weight norms in regularization; embedding norms in normalization.
thin shell
Simple: most of a high-d sphere's volume sits just under its surface. Precise: the volume of the d-ball concentrates in a shell of relative thickness shrinking as d grows; interior radii carry exponentially little volume. Intuition: high-dimensional spheres are mostly hollow. Example: random Gaussian points sit at radius ≈ √d, never deep inside. Related: concentration of measure. In practice/ML: explains distance concentration and why normalization is safe.
PCA (principal component analysis)
Simple: compress data onto its most variable directions. Precise: orthogonal projection onto the top-k eigenvectors of the covariance matrix (equivalently top-k right singular vectors of centered data). Intuition: keep the directions the data actually spreads along. Example: the notes' demo: 100 points in 1000 dims → 10 components. Related: variance budget, SVD (Class 2). In practice/ML: standard first-line dimensionality reduction.
t-SNE
Simple: a 2-D picture of high-d data that keeps neighbors together. Precise: a nonlinear projection minimizing the divergence between local neighborhood distributions. Intuition: a map for your eyes, not for measurement. Example: plotting BERT embeddings of sentences. Related: UMAP, dimensionality reduction. In practice/ML: embedding inspection; cluster sizes/gaps in the plot are artifacts.
UMAP
Simple: a faster neighbor-preserving projection that keeps more global shape. Precise: manifold-learning projection based on a fuzzy topological graph. Intuition: t-SNE's faster cousin with more global structure. Example: large embedding visualizations. Related: t-SNE. In practice/ML: modern embedding visualization (source cell 21).
overfitting
Simple: memorizing the training data instead of learning the pattern. Precise: low training error with high generalization error, enabled by excess model flexibility. Intuition: acing the practice test, failing the real one. Example: a high-d model fitting noise perfectly. Related: regularization, feature explosion. In practice/ML: a central risk the notes attach to high dimensionality.
feature selection
Simple: keep only the useful inputs. Precise: choosing a subset of features to remove irrelevant, noisy, or redundant dimensions. Intuition: fewer axes, less exponential space. Example: dropping 9,900 dead pixels' worth of features. Related: curse of dimensionality, L1 regularization. In practice/ML: improves generalization, interpretability, cost.
regularization
Simple: penalties and training tricks that prevent memorization. Precise: constraints or penalties (L1, L2), stochastic methods (dropout), and stopping rules added to training to control effective model complexity. Intuition: guardrails for a model with too much room. Example: the notes' table: L1 sparsity, L2 weight control, dropout robustness, early stopping. Related: overfitting, feature selection. In practice/ML: mandatory in high-d training regimes.
vector database
Simple: a store that finds items by embedding similarity. Precise: a system (e.g., Pinecone, Weaviate, FAISS, Milvus) indexing high-d vectors for approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval. Intuition: search by meaning, not keywords. Example: retrieving documents for RAG. Related: embedding, cosine similarity, RAG. In practice/ML: infrastructure of semantic search.
attention
Simple: each token looks at every other token and weighs how relevant it is. Precise: transformer mechanism computing softmax-normalized similarity (scaled dot products) between token representations. Intuition: a similarity-weighted average across the sequence. Example: the T in GPT. Related: dot product, semantic similarity. In practice/ML: high-d geometry affects its stability (source cell 23).
latent space
Simple: the learned compressed coordinate system behind a model's outputs. Precise: a lower-dimensional manifold where generative models (VAEs, diffusion, GANs) represent complex data. Intuition: the backstage where the real structure lives. Example: decoding a point of a VAE's space into an image. Related: embedding, representation learning. In practice/ML: generative AI operates here.
RAG (retrieval-augmented generation)
Simple: look up relevant context, then generate with it. Precise: pipeline: query → embedding → vector search over an index → retrieved context injected into the generator. Intuition: an open-book exam for an LLM. Example: chatbots grounded in company docs. Related: vector database, embedding. In practice/ML: dominant pattern for grounded generation.
semantic similarity
Simple: closeness in meaning. Precise: operationalized as geometric closeness (cosine or normalized Euclidean) of learned embeddings. Intuition: meaning measured as distance. Example: "dog" nearer "puppy" than "spreadsheet". Related: embedding, cosine similarity. In practice/ML: the quantity retrieval systems optimize.
